Distribution centre analytics — demand, labour, cost

The roster runs short
on the same days every week

Planning labour from a 28-day trailing average smooths across a weekly cycle it cannot see. The result is not random error: Friday is short on 86% of days, Sunday on none. This pipeline quantifies the gap, forecasts the demand that drives it, and prices what closing it is worth.

Fri runs short on 86% of days; Sun on 0%. A trailing average cannot distinguish a weekday from a weekend, so it under-books the peak and over-books the trough — the same way, every week. The fix is day-of-week-aware planning, not more labour.

Forecast accuracy by zone

Both models were graded on 90 days held back from training. Lower is better. The baseline simply repeats the last complete week — if the model cannot beat that, it is not earning its complexity.

Holt-Winters improves mean absolute percentage error by 17.5% overall (14.89% → 12.29%). Freezer is hardest: it swings most with the seasons and carries the smallest volume, so the same absolute miss reads as a larger percentage.

Held-back period, freezer

The hardest series to predict, over the 90 days neither model saw during training.

Both models sit above the actual line more often than below — Holt-Winters over-forecasts by 2.9% on average. That is the safer direction for a roster, since being slightly over-staffed costs wages while being short costs overtime and service. But it is a real limitation, not a design choice: a bias-corrected version would subtract the mean error, and the honest next step is to test whether that improves accuracy or simply moves the error to the expensive side.

What the mismatch costs

Weekly cost of the gap between rostered and required labour. Over-staffing is charged at the wage rate; under-staffing at an overtime premium, plus a small share treated as service failure that never recovers.
